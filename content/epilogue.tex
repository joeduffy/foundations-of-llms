\PlainChapter{Epilogue}

The papers in this volume trace a path from McCulloch and Pitts' binary threshold units to language models of hundreds of billions to trillions of parameters that exhibit few-shot learning, multi-step reasoning, tool use, and instruction following. Across that path, gradient-based optimization of differentiable parametric models---the core idea of backpropagation---remains the engine of every system discussed here; architecture, scale, and training objective changed, the underlying algorithm did not.

The prologue asked to what extent the statistical structure of language could give rise to general-purpose cognitive capabilities. The evidence assembled here answers partially. Capabilities once assumed to require explicit symbolic reasoning---few-shot learning, multi-step arithmetic, tool use, self-correction---emerged from scale and the right training signal, without being engineered in directly. What the evidence does not resolve is whether this process has a ceiling, and if so, where it lies and what would need to change to move past it.

\section*{Interpretability}

The internal representations of large transformers remain poorly understood. Mechanistic interpretability---the project of identifying specific circuits and features responsible for model behaviors---has progressed from identifying individual attention heads that perform induction, copy suppression, and indirect object identification in small models to extracting large dictionaries of interpretable features and tracing attribution graphs through production-scale models. The open problem has shifted from scale to completeness: the fraction of a model's behavior these explanations account for remains small, and verifying safety-relevant computation, as opposed to illustrating individual behaviors, remains beyond current methods. The difficulty is compounded by superposition: networks appear to represent far more features than they have dimensions, encoding concepts in overlapping directions that are difficult to disentangle. A mathematical theory of superposition and polysemanticity does not yet exist.

\section*{Open Architectural Problems}

Several concrete architectural limitations persist. Attention remains quadratic in sequence length, though hybrid designs interleaving linear-time layers with periodic full attention now run in frontier production models at million-token context, and no architecture yet matches dense-attention quality from a fixed-size recurrent state alone. Reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards has produced models that search, backtrack, and verify within their reasoning chains, but this leaves two separate questions unsettled rather than one: whether those chains faithfully reflect the underlying computation, and whether they remain monitorable as optimization pressure grows. Efficient adaptation to non-stationary data, and continual learning without catastrophic forgetting, have no real counterpart among the techniques collected in this volume.

\section*{Data, Compute, and Scaling}

The scaling laws documented in this volume predict continued performance improvements with increased resources, but the regime in which those laws hold is not unbounded. High-quality text data is finite: estimates suggest that publicly available text suitable for pretraining amounts to roughly $10^{13}$ tokens, a threshold the largest training runs have now passed, forcing reliance on repetition, lower-quality sources, and synthetic generation. Synthetic data generation, curriculum learning, and data quality filtering offer partial solutions, but whether synthetic data can substitute for natural data without distributional collapse is an open empirical question. The Chinchilla revision of the Kaplan scaling laws shifted emphasis toward data scaling, and the optimal tradeoff between pretraining compute, inference-time compute, and data quality remains an active area of investigation. Test-time scaling---allocating additional computation during inference---has become an established third axis alongside parameters and data: reasoning models trained with reinforcement learning convert search into learned behavior and expose controllable inference budgets. Allocation is now the harder question: how to divide a fixed compute budget among pretraining, post-training reinforcement learning, and inference.

\section*{Alignment}

Reinforcement learning from human feedback, constitutional AI, and direct preference optimization have produced language models that are substantially more helpful, harmless, and honest than their base pretrained counterparts. In domains admitting mechanical verification---mathematics, code, constraint satisfaction---reinforcement learning against programmatic rewards has since displaced learned preference models, sharpening the boundary of the problem: alignment in domains without verifiable ground truth still rests entirely on preference-based methods and their known failure modes. Whether these techniques scale to systems significantly more capable than their human overseers is unknown. Scalable oversight---the problem of supervising systems whose capabilities exceed those of any individual human evaluator---motivates research on debate, recursive reward modeling, and AI-assisted evaluation. The alignment problem is a problem of specification: translating human values, which are contextual, inconsistent, and incompletely understood, into mathematical objectives that can be optimized reliably. The techniques collected here supply the mechanics of alignment---reward modeling, preference learning, constitutional principles---without resolving the prior question of which values a system should hold, and on whose authority.

\section*{Agency and Reliability}

A model that answers a question and a model that acts on one differ in a specific way: the second must operate over many steps, use tools and external state, and recover when an early step fails. This volume documents the transition from scaffolded agents, in which a fixed model is prompted to call tools and reflect on its own errors, to trained agents, optimized end-to-end against the outcomes of their actions in an environment---but not the convergence of the two. Whether scaffolding remains necessary once a model is trained on long-horizon tasks, or whether training subsumes it entirely, is unresolved. Long-horizon action also exposes a failure mode absent from single-turn generation: small errors compound across steps, so that an agent reliable at each individual step can still fail the task as a whole. Bounding this compounding error, rather than only measuring task success after the fact, remains an open problem.

\section*{Conclusion}

The trajectory documented in this volume follows a recurring pattern: theoretical frameworks are proposed, computational barriers prevent their realization, and advances in hardware, algorithms, or data eventually remove those barriers, often with consequences the original authors did not foresee. Binary threshold units preceded computers powerful enough to train them by two decades; the transformer's quadratic attention preceded the hybrid architectures that made million-token context tractable by close to a decade. Whether the barrier now standing between a reasoning trace and genuine evidence of the computation that produced it falls the same way, or whether faithfulness turns out to require an architecture built for it from the start, is the question this volume leaves for the next one to answer.
